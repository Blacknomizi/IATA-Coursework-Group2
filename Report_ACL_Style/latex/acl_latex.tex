\section{Methods}

\subsection{Preliminary Clustering Analysis}

A preliminary clustering analysis was conducted to assess whether sentence embeddings exhibit natural grouping structures related to participant, intervention, and outcome fields. This stage was exploratory, aiming to evaluate whether such fields are separable in embedding space and whether this structure can inform pipeline design.

\subsubsection{Data Preparation and Label Construction}

Token-level annotations from EBM-NLP were converted into flat BIO tags (“O”, “B”, “I”). Tokens were grouped into sentences using punctuation-based segmentation (``.'', ``!'', ``?'') while preserving alignment with annotations.

Sentence-level labels were heuristically derived: a sentence was labelled as field-related if it contained a minimum number of positive BIO tokens, otherwise as “other”. This produced three binary datasets (participants, interventions, outcomes vs other). These labels are approximate, as sentences may contain multiple fields.

\subsubsection{Sentence Embeddings}

Sentences were encoded using the pretrained sentence-transformer model \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers-2019-sentence-bert} to obtain dense embeddings.

\subsubsection{Clustering and Evaluation}

Two unsupervised methods were applied: K-means (with $k=2$–$5$ for binary tasks and $k=3$ for P/I/O) and hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC). Cluster quality was evaluated by comparing cluster assignments with sentence-level labels, using the proportion of field-related sentences within each cluster.

\subsubsection{Visualisation}

Embeddings were projected to two dimensions using PCA. For each experiment, embeddings were visualised using two views: coloured by gold labels and by cluster assignments, to assess separability and clustering structure.

\subsubsection{Combined P/I/O Analysis}

A supplementary P/I/O analysis assigned each sentence to the field with the highest BIO token count, with ties labelled as mixed and empty cases as other. Mixed and other were excluded for three-way clustering, focusing on dominant field assignments. This experiment evaluated whether multiple fields can be jointly recovered from embedding structure.







\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Preliminary Clustering Results}

\paragraph{Participants vs Other}

Participant-related clustering showed weak correspondence between clusters and labels. In K-means ($k=2$–$5$), participant proportions remained low and similar across clusters (typically $\sim$15\%–21\%, with a maximum of 28.7\%), indicating no participant-dominant cluster. HAC produced a comparable pattern. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/participants_pca.png}
    \caption{PCA projection for participant-related sentences.}
    \label{fig:participants_pca}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:participants_pca}, participant and other sentences overlap extensively. Although clustering partitions the space geometrically, these regions do not align with participant labels, indicating weak separability.

\paragraph{Interventions vs Other}

Intervention-related clustering showed clearer structure. K-means identified clusters with substantially higher intervention proportions (up to $\sim$45\%), compared to $\sim$18\%–22\% in less enriched clusters. HAC showed a similar contrast (36.6\% vs 18.7\%), suggesting consistent local enrichment.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/interventions_pca.png}
    \caption{PCA projection for intervention-related sentences.}
    \label{fig:interventions_pca}
\end{figure}

Despite substantial overlap in PCA space (Figure~\ref{fig:interventions_pca}), intervention-related sentences are less uniformly distributed and form more concentrated regions than participants, indicating stronger but incomplete separability.

\paragraph{Outcomes vs Other}

Outcome-related clustering exhibited the strongest signal. K-means produced clusters with up to $\sim$52\% outcome-labelled sentences, indicating local dominance. HAC showed consistent but smaller differences (39.9\% vs 35.5\%).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/outcome_pca.png}
    \caption{PCA projection for outcome-related sentences.}
    \label{fig:outcomes_pca}
\end{figure}

Although PCA still shows substantial overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes_pca}), outcome-related content contributes more strongly to embedding structure than participants and, in some cases, interventions.

\subsubsection{Combined P/I/O Analysis}

Before filtering, the mixed class accounted for $\sim$3.2\% of sentences. After excluding mixed and other, clustering was performed on dominant P/I/O labels.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/pio_pca.png}
    \caption{PCA projection for combined P/I/O analysis.}
    \label{fig:pio_pca}
\end{figure}

K-means ($k=3$) produced well-separated geometric clusters in PCA space; however, cluster compositions remained mixed, with outcomes dominating all clusters ($\sim$47\%–58\%) and participants consistently the smallest class. HAC showed similar distributions. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pio_pca}, embedding space contains clear geometric structure, but this structure does not align with schema fields.

\subsubsection{Overall Interpretation}

Across all experiments, sentence embeddings exhibit stable geometric structure, but this structure only weakly corresponds to schema fields. Participants show minimal clustering signal, while interventions and outcomes exhibit stronger local enrichment, though none form clean, field-specific clusters.

The combined P/I/O analysis further confirms that embedding geometry does not map cleanly to schema categories. In other words, the three-way clustering essentially failed to cleanly separate participants, interventions, and outcomes. These findings suggest that unsupervised clustering alone is insufficient for schema extraction, motivating the use of explicit extraction pipelines. 

\subsubsection{Limitations}

This analysis has several limitations. Sentence-level labels were heuristically derived from token-level annotations, and sentences may contain multiple fields, introducing noise. In addition, PCA provides only a low-dimensional approximation of the embedding space.

Despite these limitations, the clustering results provide a useful empirical basis for subsequent pipeline design.
